4.1

ELIZA and the ELIZA effect

In the mid-1960s, Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT built ELIZA, published in 1966.[1][2] It had no understanding of language. It scanned each sentence for keywords, used a rule to split the sentence into parts, and used another rule to assemble a reply from them.[1][2] Its best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated a therapist who reflects a patient’s words back to them, and it swapped pronouns along the way: “You are very helpful” could become “What makes you think I am very helpful?”[1]

Weizenbaum was surprised by how people reacted. Users, including his own secretary, attributed human feelings to the program, and she reportedly asked him to leave the room so she could talk with it privately.[1] The tendency to read understanding into a computer program is now called the ELIZA effect.[1]

The program understood nothing. The people supplied the rest.

How the original worked

ELIZA scanned input from left to right for keywords. Each keyword had a rank, so a higher-ranked keyword could replace one found earlier. A decomposition rule split the sentence into parts and a reassembly rule rebuilt it into a reply. If no keyword matched, it fell back on neutral remarks or recalled earlier input.[1][2]

The rules sat in separate, editable scripts rather than in the program itself, and the 1966 paper mentions scripts in English, German and Welsh.[2] Weizenbaum listed five core problems he had to solve, including finding keywords, choosing transformations and responding when no keyword appears.[2]

Why a therapist?

The Rogerian style lets the listener appear to know little about the outside world, so the program needed no database of facts.[1][2] Weizenbaum argued that a speaker’s sense of being heard comes largely from the speaker’s own assumptions and interpretations, not from the program’s comprehension.[2] He set out to show that the communication between man and machine was superficial.[1]

Why it matters in educationModern chatbots are vastly more capable than ELIZA, but people’s readiness to read understanding into fluent text has not changed. That is a good reason to teach students to ask what a system is actually doing, not only what it appears to be saying.
4.2

Why language unlocked knowledge

ELIZA’s rules were written by hand, one pattern at a time. That approach cannot scale to a whole language, but there is another way to get language knowledge: learn it from text. Much of what people know is written down, and text has a convenient property. In any sentence, the next word is the answer to the question “what comes next?” That means text supplies its own answer key, with no one needing to label anything.

Early learning-based language systems already paid off in practical work. Recurrent networks called LSTMs were applied to speech recognition and machine translation, and in 2016 Google used them to cut translation errors in Google Translate by 60%.[4]

Text labels itself. The next word is always the answer.

Real language models use thousands or millions of tokens of context, not one word, and billions of parameters. The principle is the same: text provides the question and the answer.

Where this leads

Training a model to predict the next token in huge amounts of text is the basis of today’s chatbots. Chapter 5 returns to this idea under the name self-supervised learning.

Language as the interface

Once machines could process text and speech, applications multiplied. The LSTM article lists speech recognition, machine translation, time series analysis and handwriting recognition among its uses.[4]

Why it matters in educationStudents often ask how a chatbot “knows” things. A useful first answer is that it has read an enormous amount of text and learned what tends to come next.
4.3

RNNs and LSTMs: word order and memory limits

Language is a sequence, and order matters. A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sequence one step at a time and keeps a hidden state that acts as memory, updated from the current word and the previous state.[3] That lets it use what came before.

The weakness is that memory fades. During training, the error signals shrink exponentially as the gap between related words grows, so plain RNNs struggle to learn long-range links. This is called the vanishing gradient problem.[3] In 1997, Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber published the paper usually cited for the long short-term memory network, or LSTM (earlier versions date to 1995–96), which adds a memory cell and three gates that control what to forget, what to store and what to pass on.[3][4]

Reading one word at a time means the start of a long sentence fades.

A second problem appeared in translation. Standard encoder-decoder models squeezed a whole sentence into a single fixed-length vector, which Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio called a bottleneck in 2014. Their fix let the model search the source sentence for the most relevant words while producing each translated word.[5] That idea, attention, is where the story goes next.

What the bars mean

“Remaining memory” is how much of the original clue is still available when the question arrives. In the plain RNN it fades a little at every word, so it shrinks fast with distance. The LSTM-style bar fades four times more slowly, standing in for the gates that help the network hold on. Attention, shown in the next sections, lets the model look directly at the clue however far back it is.[3][4][7]

The other cost: waiting in line

RNNs process tokens one after another, so their steps cannot be computed in parallel the way Chapter 3’s parallel work can.[6] Chapter 3’s parallel-versus-sequential idea applies here directly.

Why it matters in educationIt explains an everyday experience: why early chatbots and translators lost the thread in long passages. The fix was not more memory but a different way of looking back.
4.4

Tokens, embeddings, and vectors

A network works with numbers, not letters, so text goes through two conversions. First, the text is cut into pieces called tokens. A token can be a single character, a digit or punctuation mark, or a whole word, and for language models it is usually part of a word.[8] A popular method for language-model tokenizers starts from single characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pairs.[8]

Second, each token is mapped to a list of numbers called a vector. This is its embedding, and words used in similar contexts end up with vectors close together.[9][10] Google’s word2vec, presented in 2013, made this practical at scale.[9]

Meaning becomes a location.

Counting tokens

Context limits are measured in tokens, not words. As a rough rule, one word is about 1.5 tokens, but it varies by model and tokenizer, and some languages are less efficient. In IBM’s example, a Telugu sentence with far fewer characters than its English equivalent produced over seven times as many tokens.[13]

What a vocabulary does

The vocabulary size sets how finely text is split: a larger vocabulary gives longer tokens and a smaller one gives more fragments. A character-only vocabulary cannot encode a symbol it has never seen, so tokenizers use an “unknown” token or work on raw bytes so that any text can be encoded.[8]

Why it matters in educationTokens explain odd chatbot behavior, such as miscounting letters in a word, and why the same message can cost more in one language than another. Embeddings explain why a chatbot treats “car” and “automobile” as close relatives.

4.5

Semantic similarity and context

Once words are points on a map, similarity becomes distance. Words that appear in similar contexts end up near each other, so the model can treat “happy” and “glad” as relatives without being told.[9][10] That is how a system trained only on text picks up a working sense of meaning.

There were two limits. First, early embeddings were static: each word had one vector regardless of the sentence, so a word with several meanings had to share a single vector. Later models such as ELMo and BERT gave words context-dependent vectors.[9] Second, a language model can only look at a limited amount of text at once, called its context window. It is measured in tokens, and anything outside it is invisible to the model unless it is summarized, retrieved or provided again.[12]

A model can only use what is in front of it.

Window sizes

By the mid-2020s, long-context systems reported windows from hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens, and Google researchers reported retrieval tests at up to 10 million.[12] A bigger window does not guarantee that everything in it is used equally: one study found performance often dropped when the relevant information sat in the middle of a long input rather than near the start or end.[12]

Why not make it huge?

Bigger windows need more computing power, and the need grows quadratically, so doubling the number of input tokens takes about four times the processing. Longer contexts can also slow responses.[13]

Why it matters in educationThis explains why a long chat can “forget” details from the start, and why pasting the key document into the conversation works better than hoping the model remembers it.

Explainers

4.6

Self-attention and the transformer (2017)

In June 2017, eight researchers published “Attention Is All You Need.” It proposed the Transformer, a network “based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely.”[6]

The core idea is self-attention. Every token looks at every other token in the text and decides how much each one matters to it. Its new representation is a weighted mix of all the others, with the weights coming from a function called softmax, which turns raw scores into percentages that add up to 100%.[6][7][11] In “the animal didn’t cross the street because it was too tired,” the word “it” can lean heavily on “animal.”

Instead of reading left to right and hoping to remember, every word looks at every other word at once.

Because each token’s computation does not wait for the one before it, all of them can be computed at the same time.[6] That fits the parallel hardware of Chapter 3. The paper reported a state-of-the-art English-to-French translation score after 3.5 days of training on eight GPUs.[6]

The three roles: query, key and value

Each token is converted into a query, a key and a value using learned weights. The token’s new representation is a weighted sum of all tokens’ values, and each weight reflects how well its query matches the other token’s key. Multi-head attention runs several of these computations side by side and combines the results.[6][7]

Attention came first as an add-on

Earlier sequence models were mostly LSTMs and similar recurrent networks, with attention used only as an extra. The Transformer paper’s central claim was that attention could be the only mechanism needed to relate input and output.[6][7]

Why it matters in educationAttention explains why a chatbot can connect a pronoun to its noun, or a question to a sentence three paragraphs back. It is also the design behind nearly every chatbot students use today.

Explainers

From section 4.6Self-attention

Self-attention lets each token in a sequence weigh every other token when forming its own representation. Tokens whose keys match its query count for more.[6][7]

  1. Score. Compare this token’s query with every token’s key.
  2. Convert. Turn the scores into weights with softmax.[11]
  3. Mix. Add up all the tokens’ values, each scaled by its weight.[7]

Softmax turns a list of numbers into probabilities. It raises e to each number and divides each result by the total, so every output is between 0 and 1 and all of them add up to 1. Larger inputs get larger shares.[11]

From section 4.6Transformer

The Transformer is a network design built on attention instead of recurrence. It was introduced in 2017 by Vaswani and colleagues.[6]

PartJob
EncoderProduces a representation for each input token
DecoderGenerates output tokens. Its self-attention is masked, so it can only look at earlier tokens

The overview lists several reasons it became dominant: pretraining on large unlabeled text, then fine-tuning; use beyond text for images, audio and more; easy-to-use software frameworks and fast hardware; and GPT-style generative models refined with human feedback, which produced ChatGPT and drove wide adoption.[7]
