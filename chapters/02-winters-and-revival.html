2.1

The XOR problem and the first winter

By the late 1960s the perceptron had become a symbol of what learning machines might do. In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a book, Perceptrons, that mapped out what a single layer of such units cannot do.[1] The standard example is XOR, short for “exclusive or”: the answer is yes when exactly one of two inputs is on, but not when both are. No single threshold unit can get this right (my explanation of the problem, which the book names among its examples[1]), and nobody yet had a widely used method to train networks with more than one layer.[2]

The book is often blamed for what came next, but the money moved for several reasons at once. In the US, the 1969 and 1973 Mansfield Amendments limited military research funding to projects with a direct military use, the 1973 one explicitly for ARPA.[2][4] In 1974, DARPA cancelled a speech-recognition contract worth $3 million a year after its program fell short of its goals.[2] In the UK, a 1973 report by Sir James Lighthill judged that AI had failed to meet its “grandiose objectives,” and the government largely ended support for AI research in British universities.[2][3]

A book, a report and a budget line. The first winter had more than one cause.

The result is what people now call an AI winter: a period of reduced funding and interest after a stretch of hype.[2] Money for neural network research was especially hard to find in the 1970s.[2]

What the book actually proved

The central proofs concern restricted, local single-layer perceptrons. For example, they cannot compute parity, and the book names XOR among the patterns it discusses. The number of inputs each unit must see grows with the size of the picture when the question is whether a shape is connected.[1] Rosenblatt had shown that an elementary perceptron with an unlimited hidden layer could solve any classification problem. The book’s analysis used the restricted version.[1]

In an expanded 1988 edition, the authors called multilayer networks a “sterile” extension and wrote in a later chapter that little of significance had changed since 1969.[1]

Did the book end neural network research?

It is often thought to have caused a decline in neural network research in the 1970s and early 1980s.[1] The authors said research waned because of problems inherent in the approach, not because of their book. One critic argued that a narrow proof about one issue was read as an attack on the whole idea. In 1986, Rumelhart and colleagues said the authors’ pessimism about multilayer learning was misplaced.[1]

The historian Thomas Haigh disputes that there was a true winter in the 1970s, pointing to rapid growth in membership of the ACM’s special interest group on AI during those years.[2] Both views are worth knowing.

The Lighthill report in more detail

The Science Research Council commissioned it in 1972 as a personal review of the field, and Lighthill completed it in July, and it was published in 1973.[3] Its verdict: “In no part of the field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then promised.” His main technical point was combinatorial explosion, meaning that techniques that work on small problems do not scale to realistic ones. He judged robot building a failure, saw success in advanced automation only in restricted domains, and judged work on modelling the nervous system the most successful category.[3]

A public debate followed on May 9, 1973, at the Royal Institution in London, where Lighthill faced Donald Michie, John McCarthy and Richard Gregory.[3] After the report, UK work continued mainly at Edinburgh, Essex and Sussex.[2]

Why it matters in educationA technical limit, a funding policy and a loss of confidence arrived together. When someone says AI “failed” in the 1970s, a good follow-up is to ask which part they mean.

From section 2.1AI winter

An AI winter is a period of reduced funding and interest in AI research. The pattern described is a cycle: hype, disappointment and criticism, funding cuts, and renewed interest years or decades later.[2]

The term first appeared in 1984, in a public debate at the annual meeting of the AAAI. Roger Schank and Marvin Minsky, who had lived through the 1970s downturn, warned that enthusiasm in the 1980s had spiraled out of control and that a chain reaction of pessimism could follow.[2]

2.2

Backpropagation revived

Backpropagation is the method that made networks with several layers trainable. The idea has four steps, repeated over many examples:

  1. Guess. The network turns the inputs into an output.
  2. Measure the error. Compare the output with the correct answer. The gap is the error.[6]
  3. Send the blame backward. Work out, layer by layer from the output back to the input, how much each weight contributed to the error.[6]
  4. Nudge. Adjust each weight a little in the direction that shrinks the error.[6]

Guess, measure the error, send the blame backward, adjust.

In October 1986, David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams published a paper in Nature describing the procedure. They showed that the “hidden” units, the ones that are neither inputs nor outputs, come to represent useful features of the task.[5][6] The single-layer perceptron of Chapter 1 had no hidden units to train.

The method was not new in 1986. Related ideas appeared in control theory from the 1950s, the core calculation was published in 1970 by Seppo Linnainmaa, and Paul Werbos applied it to multilayer networks in 1982. The 1986 paper is the one that popularized it.[6]

Why Rosenblatt could not use it

Rosenblatt’s neurons gave outputs in discrete steps, and the slope of such an output is zero.[6] Backpropagation needs to know how a small change in a weight changes the result. A hard yes-or-no threshold, like the one in Chapter 1’s dinner demo, gives no such information. Networks that use smooth outputs do.

Strictly speaking

Backpropagation, in the strict sense, is only the calculation of how the error depends on each weight, done efficiently with the chain rule from calculus. In everyday use the word often covers the whole training loop, which adds a step that moves the weights, such as gradient descent.[6] Gradient descent is not guaranteed to find the best possible weights, only a locally good set.[6]

What the 1986 paper claimed

The abstract says the procedure repeatedly adjusts the connection weights to reduce the difference between the actual output and the desired output. As a result, hidden units come to represent important features of the task. The authors present this ability to build new useful features as what separates it from earlier, simpler methods such as the perceptron-convergence procedure.[5] I read the abstract record, not the full paper.

Why it matters in educationWhen people say a model was “trained,” this is the engine behind the word. Later chapters meet it again at a much larger scale.

From section 2.2Supervised learning

Supervised learning trains a model on examples that already include the correct answers. Each example pairs an input with a label, which serves as the “ground truth.” A loss function measures the gap between the model’s prediction and the label, and training adjusts the model to shrink it.[14]

2.3

LeNet reads the mail

In 1989, a research group at AT&T Bell Laboratories around Yann LeCun made the first practical application of backpropagation to a convolutional network, a design built for images. Their task was reading handwritten digits, using ZIP codes from US mail that passed through the Buffalo, New York post office.[7] The family of networks became known as LeNet.

The key idea: instead of connecting every pixel to every unit, a small filter slides across the image and checks each patch for the same feature, such as an edge. The same filter weights are reused everywhere, so the network needs far fewer weights and can spot a feature wherever it appears.[8]

Learning from examples had left the lab and was reading real mail and real checks.

Later versions went into products. Bell Labs loaded a LeNet onto a signal-processing chip that let ATMs read check amounts, and NCR, which AT&T acquired in 1991, deployed similar systems in bank back-office check readers from June 1996.[7] One 2001 estimate put the volume at about 20 million checks a day, roughly a tenth of all US checks.[7]

How a filter works

A filter is a small set of learnable weights plus a bias. It moves across the width and height of the image, computing a weighted sum with each patch it covers. The step size is called the stride. The results form a feature map, and stacking the maps from many filters gives the layer’s output.[8]

Weight sharing is what makes this efficient. For a 100×100 image, one fully connected unit would need 10,000 weights, while a shared 5×5 filter needs only 25.[8] Stacked layers combine small features into larger ones.[8]

Where the idea came from

In 1980, Kunihiko Fukushima introduced the neocognitron, which already used shared-weight layers and downsampling layers. In 1989, LeCun and colleagues used backpropagation to learn the filter values directly from images of handwritten digits, which made the learning automatic and outperformed hand-designed filters.[8]

2.4

The second winter

Chapter 1 ended with rule-based systems. In the 1980s they became a business. Expert systems capture a specialist’s knowledge as if-then rules in a knowledge base, and an inference engine applies the rules to facts.[10] MYCIN diagnosed infectious diseases and R1 configured DEC VAX computers.[10] Corporate spending on AI topped a billion dollars by 1985.[2]

Then it fell apart in stages. Specialized Lisp machines, built to run the AI language Lisp, lost out to cheaper general-purpose workstations and desktops. A half-billion-dollar industry was effectively replaced within a single year, 1987.[2] DARPA, which had restarted AI funding in response to Japan’s Fifth Generation project, made deep cuts after a change of leadership in 1987.[2] Expert systems themselves proved costly to maintain, brittle, and unable to learn.[2]

The warning came in 1984. The collapse began in 1987.

The term “AI winter” dates from 1984, when Roger Schank and Marvin Minsky warned a conference that enthusiasm had outrun reality. Three years later the billion-dollar AI industry began to collapse.[2]

Two readings of the decline

The same article gives two explanations. One is that the systems failed to live up to their hyped promise. The other is that they succeeded so well that rule engines became standard tools and the label “expert system” faded. In the 2000s the technology returned as “rule-based systems” inside business software, and researchers came to see machine learning as necessary for overcoming the original limits.[10]

What happened to the Lisp machine makers

Lisp Machines, Inc. was founded in 1979 by Richard Greenblatt of MIT’s AI Lab, and a rival firm, Symbolics, was started after a 1979 dispute at MIT’s AI Lab. LMI went bankrupt in 1987 before its next machine reached market. Symbolics’ 3600 shipped a year late.[11] That article does not itself explain the wider market collapse. The explanation, cheaper general-purpose machines catching up, comes from the AI winter article.[2]

Dates are approximate

The AI winter article places the second winter at about 1987–2000, and says enthusiasm and optimism generally increased from the field’s low point in the early 1990s.[2] Treat both endpoints as soft.

Why it matters in educationNotice what the second winter hit: a business built on hand-written rules and special-purpose hardware. In the same years, a learning system was reading checks. The lesson is less “AI failed” than “one approach and one business model ran out of steam.”
2.5

Deep Blue vs. Kasparov: rules against learning

In February 1996, IBM’s chess computer Deep Blue lost a six-game match to world champion Garry Kasparov, 4 to 2, although it won Game 1, the first time a computer beat a reigning world champion in a game under regular time controls.[12][13] After an upgrade, the May 1997 rematch in New York went the other way. Deep Blue won 3½ to 2½, taking the deciding Game 6.[12][13] It examined about 200 million chess positions per second.[12]

What it did not do was learn. Deep Blue’s evaluation function was designed by hand, its opening moves came from a library built with grandmasters, and its strength came from searching enormous numbers of positions.[13] The Wikipedia article describes it as an expert system and an example of symbolic AI whose strength came mainly from computing power, not machine learning.[13]

The decade’s best-known machine victory belonged to hand-built rules and brute-force search.

Meanwhile, learning systems were quietly reading ZIP codes and checks. The two approaches were doing different jobs, and neither had won.

How it worked

The 1997 version searched about 200 million positions per second, roughly twice the 1996 version. Wikipedia describes the hardware as an IBM RS/6000 SP with 30 PowerPC processors and 480 custom chess chips that sped up the search. Typical search depth was six to eight moves ahead, and sometimes twenty or more. The developers estimated each extra level of depth added 50 to 70 points of playing strength on the Elo rating scale.[13]

IBM’s own page describes 32 processors and a speed of 11.38 billion floating-point operations per second.[12] The two accounts differ on the processor count, and I did not resolve the difference.

The evaluation function was hand-designed. Its parameters were set by analyzing thousands of master games, and it was split into about 8,000 parts, many for special positions. The opening book held over 4,000 positions drawn from 700,000 grandmaster games. The grandmasters Miguel Illescas, John Fedorowicz and Nick de Firmian supplied the opening library, and Joel Benjamin helped fine-tune its rules before the 1997 match.[13]

The controversy

In Game 2, Kasparov said some moves showed unusual creativity and suggested human intervention. IBM denied this and said the only human involvement was between games. Kasparov asked for a rematch, which IBM refused, and for the log files, which IBM at first refused and later published online. The developers said they used the between-game adjustments the rules allowed. A bug caused a seemingly random move at move 44 of Game 1, which Kasparov took as a sign of superior intelligence.[13]

What IBM said it showed

IBM presents the result as an inflection point in computing, widely seen at the time as a symbolic test of whether supercomputers were catching up with human intelligence. It says the underlying technology advanced work in drug discovery, financial risk assessment, pattern discovery in large databases and gene research.[12]

What came after

Modern chess engines such as Leela Chess Zero train neural networks through reinforcement learning, a different path from Deep Blue’s.[13] Chapter 3 returns to game-playing systems that do learn.

Why it matters in educationDeep Blue plays chess and nothing else, which is the narrow AI idea from Chapter 1. Beating a champion at one game says little about general intelligence. The better question to ask is how it won.
