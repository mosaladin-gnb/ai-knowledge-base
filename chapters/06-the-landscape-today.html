6.1

Adoption and investment

Stanford’s 2026 AI Index reports that 88% of organizations worldwide used AI in 2025, up from 55% in 2023, with Europe at 91% and North America at 90%.[2] It also says generative AI reached 53% adoption within three years, faster than the internet or computers did at the same stage.[1][2]

Money followed. Corporate AI investment hit a record $581.69 billion in 2025, made up of $344.66 billion in private investment and $214.44 billion in mergers and acquisitions.[2] In the report’s country comparison, the United States accounted for $285.88 billion, Europe for $20.92 billion and China for $12.41 billion.[1][2]

AI went from a pilot project to a default in about three years.

6.2

Closed source vs. open weights

Some models stay on the company’s servers, and you reach them only through an app or a service. Others are released as open weights, meaning the model’s parameters are published so anyone can download and run them. The training data and code are usually not included.[4]

That is different from “open source AI.” The Open Source Initiative’s definition, version 1.0 from October 28, 2024, says an open-source AI system must let people use, study, modify and share it. That requires the parameters, the complete code used to train and run it, and enough detail about the training data for a skilled person to rebuild a similar system.[3] Critics call open-weight releases that use the open-source label “openwashing.”[4]

Open weights means you can run it. Open source means you can rebuild it.

Examples, as the sources describe them

DeepSeek’s V3 and R1 models were released as open-weight models under the MIT license.[4][5] Mistral’s Mixtral was released under the Apache 2.0 license.[7] Meta describes Llama as open source, but that is disputed: its license restricts some uses and the origin of its training data has not been disclosed.[4]

Why license wording matters

Licenses vary. Restrictive terms can deter companies that fear future lawsuits or changes to the terms, and many smaller models specify no license at all.[4]

Why it matters in education“Free to download” is not the same as “free to use however you like,” and neither guarantees student data stays private. Ask what the license permits and where the model actually runs.

6.3

Efficient design and the Chinese labs

In January 2025, a Chinese company called DeepSeek, founded in 2023 in Hangzhou and funded by a hedge fund, released the reasoning model R1 and a chatbot. By January 27 its app had passed ChatGPT as the most downloaded free app on the US iPhone store, and Wikipedia says Nvidia’s share price fell 18% as a result.[5] The surprise was cost. DeepSeek’s earlier V3 model took 2.788 million H800 GPU hours to train, which the company claims cost about $6 million, a figure that Wikipedia’s DeepSeek article says covers only part of the true cost.[5][6]

One design choice behind efficient models like it is called mixture of experts. Instead of using every part of a huge network for every word, a small router picks a few specialized sub-networks to handle each token. V3 has 671 billion parameters in total but uses only 37 billion for any one token.[6][8]

A very large model that only wakes up the parts it needs.

The catch

All the experts must still be held in memory even though only a few run at a time, so memory savings are limited. Training is also trickier, because a router can overuse a few experts and leave others under-trained.[8]

Where the Chinese labs stand

The 2026 AI Index reports Arena scores of 1,503 for the top US model and 1,464 for the top Chinese model, a gap of 39 points.[1] In investment, the report’s comparison lists $285.88 billion for the United States and $12.41 billion for China.[1][2] Both statements describe snapshots of the 2025–2026 period. This chapter does not try to name a current leader.

Why it matters in educationEfficiency changes who can build and run these systems. A model that is cheaper to run is easier for a district, a university or a smaller country to host itself.

6.4

What subscriptions cost

As of October 26, 2026, the three best-known consumer chatbots follow a similar pattern: a free tier, a standard paid tier at about $20 a month, and much pricier tiers for heavy use. ChatGPT Plus is $20 a month, with Pro tiers at $100 and $200.[18] Claude Pro is $20 a month, or $17 a month billed annually, and Max starts at $100.[16] Google’s AI Plus is $4.99, AI Pro is $19.99, and AI Ultra is $99.99 or $199.99.[19]

For teachers there is a notable exception. OpenAI lists ChatGPT for Teachers as free for verified US K–12 educators through June 2027.[17]

About $20 a month buys the standard tier. Heavy use costs five to ten times that.

Disclosure: Claude is made by Anthropic. Its prices are reported on the same terms as the others, from the company’s own pricing page.

6.5

Infrastructure and energy

Training and running these models happens in data centres full of GPUs, and they use a lot of electricity. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, roughly 1.5% of the world’s electricity. The United States accounted for 45% of that, China 25% and Europe 15%.[15]

The IEA projects consumption could reach around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, slightly more than Japan’s total electricity use today.[15] It also warns of large uncertainties. Its base case for 2035 is about 1,200 terawatt-hours, within a range of 700 to 1,700 across scenarios.[15]

AI’s appetite for power is real, growing, and hard to forecast.

Where the growth lands

In the United States, data centres account for nearly half of electricity demand growth to 2030. In advanced economies they account for more than 20% of demand growth, and in emerging and developing economies around 5%.[15] The IEA’s “high efficiency” case puts 2035 demand 20% below its base case.[15]

Careful with the numbers

The IEA figures cover data centres overall, of which AI is a fast-growing part. The report is from 2025, so the later years are projections, not measurements.[15]

Why it matters in education“How much energy does one chatbot question use?” is a common student question, and it has no simple answer. The honest summary is that the totals are large and growing, and the forecasts vary widely.
6.6

Reasoning models, multimodal AI, and agents

Three capabilities define the current generation. Reasoning models spend extra computing time working through a problem step by step before they answer. OpenAI’s o1-preview in September 2024 popularized the label, and DeepSeek-R1 followed in January 2025.[9][10] Multimodal models take more than text. GPT-4, reported in March 2023, accepted images as well as text.[11] Agents go further and act: they plan a goal into steps, call tools such as search or other software, and keep going without a person at each step.[12]

Each comes with a catch. Reasoning models can be much slower and costlier. A Wikipedia summary reports a study on a math benchmark that found them 10 to 74 times more expensive to run, though the article does not name the study.[10] GPT-4 remained less capable than humans in many real-world situations.[11] Agents can loop without progress, and unsupervised ones that handle customer data raise security concerns.[12]

Think longer, see more, and take actions. Each one raises the stakes.

How agents are described

IBM describes an agent as a system that plans, uses tools, remembers earlier interactions and acts with some autonomy. It recommends activity logs, the ability to interrupt, and human approval for high-impact actions.[12] On the OSWorld benchmark, agents’ accuracy at operating a computer rose from about 5% in 2024 to 66.3% in 2025, still under the 72.35% human baseline.[1]

A note on the multimodal claims

GPT-4’s report said it scored near the top 10% of test takers on a simulated bar exam, and also that it was still less capable than humans in many real-world situations. Both statements come from the same report.[11]

Why it matters in educationThe key question for any agent is which actions it may take without asking. Reading a handbook is low risk. Emailing every parent is not.

Explainers

From section 6.6Reasoning model

A reasoning model is a language model trained to solve multi-step problems, revisiting and revising earlier steps and spending extra time thinking before it answers. The extra computing done at answer time is called test-time compute.[10]

DeepSeek’s R1 paper reports that reasoning can be encouraged through reinforcement learning, without human-written reasoning examples, and that behaviors such as self-reflection and verification emerged.[9]

From section 6.6Agent

An AI agent autonomously carries out tasks by planning steps and using available tools.[12]

  1. Plan. Break a goal into subtasks.[12]
  2. Think, act, observe. Use tools to gather what is missing, then revise the plan.[12]
  3. Reflect. Store outcomes and feedback to improve later.[12]

Listed risks include infinite loops, computational cost, and privacy and security concerns when agents handle business or customer data.[12]

6.7

Chips, data sovereignty, and geopolitics

AI is now an issue between governments. In October 2022, the United States restricted exports of advanced AI chips made with American inputs to China. An October 2023 update tightened the rules, covering near-miss chips and adding companies to a restricted list.[20] AI models themselves can be treated the same way. In June 2026 the US government ordered Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, to bar foreign nationals from two of its models, and access was cut off. The Commerce Department lifted the controls, and Anthropic said it would begin restoring access from July 1, 2026.[21]

For schools, the more everyday version is data sovereignty: the idea that data is subject to the laws of the country or region where it was generated.[22] Where a chatbot processes a student’s words, and whose laws apply to them, depends on the provider as well as on the physical server.[22]

AI is infrastructure now, and infrastructure has borders.

Disclosure: Anthropic, the company in the June 2026 episode, makes Claude. The account here follows a news report, not the company’s own statement, and the government gave no specific reason beyond unspecified national security concerns.[21]

What the chip rules did

The October 2023 update changed the key measure to performance density, created a “gray zone” of chips that fell below the thresholds but undercut the rule’s intent, and limited exports to companies headquartered in China, Macau and arms-embargoed countries. It also added 13 entities to the restricted list.[20] The CSIS analysis I used describes the rules and does not judge how well they have worked.

Residency versus sovereignty

IBM separates the terms. Sovereignty means data is stored and processed in the country where it was generated. Residency means it is stored in a different country. It adds that the US CLOUD Act lets US authorities compel US-based companies to hand over data they control even when it is stored abroad, so jurisdiction can depend on the provider, not only the server.[22] IBM also points to the EU’s GDPR as an example of a privacy regulation.[22]

Why it matters in educationWhen a district adopts an AI tool, student data may cross borders. A few plain questions about where data goes, who can reach it, and how long it is kept belong in every procurement conversation. Chapter 7 covers the law on student data.
