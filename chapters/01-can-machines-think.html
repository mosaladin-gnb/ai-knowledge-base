1.1

Turing’s question

In 1950, the mathematician Alan Turing opened a paper with a simple question: can machines think?[1] He replaced it with a more precisely worded question and proposed a practical test instead, now called the Turing test. A judge chats in text with two hidden partners, one human and one machine. If the judge cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine passes.[2]

Judge the machine by what it does, not by what it is.

The original game was different

In Turing’s own version, the hidden players were a man and a woman, and the judge tried to tell which was which. Turing then asked what would happen if a machine took one of the parts.[1][2] The chatbot version above is the standard reading, and the one most people mean today.[2]

Passing is not proof of understanding

Philosophers have long argued that a system could give convincing replies by brute force, for example by looking up pre-written answers, without any real intelligence. Ned Block’s “Blockhead” thought experiment makes this point. So passing the test does not logically guarantee intelligence.[2]

Why it matters in educationModern chatbots make this old test feel close. That is a good reason to teach the question of whether convincing replies show understanding, instead of settling it by gut feeling.

1.2

Rule-based AI: if this, then that

The first approach to machine intelligence was to write the intelligence down by hand: explicit if-then rules that a person composes and a machine follows.[5]

In 1955, four researchers (McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon) proposed a summer study at Dartmouth College. It rested on one idea: any feature of learning or intelligence can be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it. Their proposal introduced the term artificial intelligence.[3][4]

A rule-based machine knows exactly what it was told, and nothing else.

A student who writes “the water turns into vapor and then back into drops” has the right idea but no keywords, and is marked wrong. A student who strings the two words into nonsense is marked right.

How far did rules go?

Symbolic, rule-based AI dominated the field from the mid-1950s to the mid-1990s. Its first commercially successful form was the expert system: a knowledge base of hand-written rules plus an engine that applies them. Systems such as DENDRAL, MYCIN and XCON showed real value, and companies adopted them widely in the 1980s. But large rule bases were costly to build and maintain, and the systems were brittle outside their narrow domains, which contributed to the second AI winter.[5]

About the Dartmouth proposal

The proposal, dated August 31, 1955, called for a two-month study with ten participants in summer 1956. It listed research areas, including neural nets and machines that improve themselves.[3] The term is often credited to the 1956 workshop itself. These sources place it in the 1955 proposal, and credit McCarthy with choosing the name.[3][4]

Looking aheadThe right-hand column of the table is what the perceptron, next, tried to build. Chapter 2 picks up what happened when that approach met its limits.
1.3

The perceptron (1957)

Frank Rosenblatt was a psychologist at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory in Buffalo, New York, interested in the minimum a brain needs in order to do what it does.[9] His design, the perceptron, was first simulated in 1957 on an IBM 704 computer, and was later built as a custom machine, the Mark I Perceptron, with Navy funding.[10] The Mark I is now at the Smithsonian.[9]

A perceptron does three things:

  1. Takes inputs. Numbers describing what it is looking at, such as how bright each point in an image is.
  2. Weighs them. Each input is multiplied by a weight, a number saying how much that input matters.
  3. Compares to a threshold. It adds everything up. If the total clears the threshold the answer is yes, otherwise no.[7][10]

Nobody wrote the rules. The machine adjusted its own weights from examples.

That is the break with rule-based AI. The Mark I was a supervised image classifier, trained on examples with known answers instead of being programmed with rules.[11] The catch: it had a single layer, and Rosenblatt did not yet know how to train networks with several.[9] Chapter 2 begins there.

What it actually did

At the Navy’s July 1958 demonstration, an IBM 704 fed punched cards learned, after 50 trials, to tell cards marked on the left from cards marked on the right.[9] The Mark I hardware was designed for image recognition.[10] Experiments with the Mark I included telling simple shapes and letters apart, such as squares from circles and X from E.[11]

When the Mark I was built is not settled. One source gives 1958, another has the team assembling and testing it between June and December 1959.[11][10]

What it lacked

A single layer can only learn simple patterns. In 1969, Minsky and Papert showed that single-layer perceptrons cannot learn certain patterns, with XOR as the standard example. The book contributed to a significant decline in neural network research and funding that lasted about a decade.[10] That is where Chapter 2 starts.

From section 1.3Weight

A weight is a number that says how much one input matters to a decision. In the biological comparison, it plays the role of the strength of a connection between neurons.[7]

WeightEffect
Large and positiveStrongly pushes the decision toward yes
Close to zeroThe input is effectively ignored
NegativePushes against yes

Nobody sets the weights by hand in a learning system. Training adjusts them. Modern models have far more than the three in the demo, and later chapters cover how many. When people say a model has “open weights,” they mean the file of these learned numbers is public.

From section 1.3Threshold

A threshold is the bar the weighted total must clear before the unit says yes. Think of a pass mark: 59 and 60 are nearly the same score, but one passes and one does not.

Modern descriptions often fold this into a bias term, which plays a role similar to a biological firing threshold. The result then goes through an activation function. The step function is the hard yes-or-no version; sigmoid and ReLU are common smoother ones.[7]

The artificial neuron is a deliberate simplification meant to capture a few features of real neurons. It does not reproduce them faithfully.[7]

The 1943 McCulloch and Pitts version used binary on/off states, whole-number weights and a step-function threshold.[8] A single unit like this could implement AND and OR, but not XOR. Networks of such units could build any logical function.[7][8]

1.4

Hype and funding

In July 1958 the Navy unveiled the perceptron, and Rosenblatt’s statements drew heavy coverage from reporters.[9] A Navy-organized press conference that year is also on record.[10] The New York Times ran the headline “New Navy Device Learns by Doing,”[9] and reported that the Navy expected such machines eventually to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce themselves and be conscious of their own existence.[10]

Read a 1958 headline aloud without the date and it could be from this year.

The pattern is worth remembering: big claims, big money, hard technical limits, then disappointment. The next chapter shows what happened when the limits arrived.

A controversy from day one

Rosenblatt’s statements caused a heated controversy in the young AI community.[10] The New Yorker described it as a serious rival to the human brain.[9] The research was Navy-funded, and the press conference was organized by the Navy.[10]

The cost of the cycle

The 1969 critique of single-layer perceptrons contributed to a significant decline in neural network research and funding that lasted about a decade.[10] Chapter 2 follows that story.

Why it matters in educationTeachers who feel they are living through unprecedented hype are in good company. The excitement is real, and so is a history of promises outrunning what the technology could do. That makes a calm, factual way into a skeptical discussion.
