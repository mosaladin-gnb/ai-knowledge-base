5.1

GPT-1 to GPT-3: the scale story

“GPT” stands for generative pre-trained transformer.[4] The first one, GPT-1, was released by OpenAI in June 2018 with 117 million parameters. It was trained in two stages: first it learned language from raw text, then it was adapted to individual tasks.[1] GPT-2 followed in 2019 with 1.5 billion parameters, and OpenAI at first held the full model back because of concerns about misuse.[2]

In 2020 came GPT-3, with 175 billion parameters, which its authors called ten times more than any previous non-sparse language model.[3] Its surprise was how it could be used. It was given a task and a few examples written as ordinary text, with no retraining, and it often performed well. This is called few-shot learning.[3] The authors’ main finding was that scaling up a language model greatly improved this kind of general, task-agnostic performance.[3]

Same design, far more parameters, and a new behavior: it could be taught by showing, not retraining.

What the authors also reported

GPT-2 was trained on WebText, about 8 million documents from web pages and roughly 40 GB of text with Wikipedia pages removed.[2] The GPT-3 paper noted datasets where few-shot learning still struggled, raised methodological issues with training on large web collections, and found that people had difficulty telling its sample news articles from human-written ones.[3]

Why it matters in educationFew-shot learning is why showing a chatbot one or two examples of what you want works better than describing it loosely. That is the practical root of good prompting, covered in Chapter 7.
5.2

Predicting the next word: self-supervised pre-training

Chapter 3 described supervised learning, which needs humans to label examples, and unsupervised learning, which finds patterns with no answers. Self-supervised learning sits between them. It builds its own labels out of unlabeled data, so no human has to write the answers.[5]

For language there are two common versions. GPT-style models predict the next word and use the real next word as the correct answer. BERT-style models hide random words and predict those instead.[5] GPT-3 was trained primarily this way.[5] The result of this long first stage is called pre-training. It is the phase where the model’s weights are adjusted from errors. Using the finished model later to produce answers is called inference.[6]

Every sentence is its own exam and its own answer key.

A book of 100,000 words yields about 100,000 exercises, with nobody labeling anything. That is how a model can learn from a large share of the internet.

Pretext and downstream tasks

The exercise the model practices on, such as guessing a hidden word, is called a pretext task. What it learns is then reused for a downstream task, often after fine-tuning with a small amount of labeled data.[5]

Why not just use labels?

Labels are expensive. Chapter 3’s ImageNet needed tens of thousands of human workers. Text, by contrast, already contains its own answers, which is why this approach could be pushed to such a large scale.

Why it matters in educationIt answers “where did it learn that?” A chatbot’s fluency comes from predicting words across an enormous amount of text, not from anyone programming grammar rules.

From section 5.2Training vs. inference

Training is the phase where a network learns from data. Its weights are repeatedly adjusted based on feedback about errors, which takes heavy computing. Inference is the phase where the trained model applies what it learned to new inputs. NVIDIA’s analogy is school for training and the job for inference.[6]

From section 5.2Weights inside a language model

A weight is a number that says how strongly one unit’s output counts for the next unit. Training adjusts these numbers based on feedback about errors.[6] When training ends they are frozen, so every answer the model gives is computed from the same fixed set. (The first sentence and the frozen-weights point are general background, not from a cited page. Chapter 1’s weight explainer shows a single weight at work.)

Simplified idea · 6 picturesWhat a weight isThe numbers training adjusts, and what they do inside a language model.See it in pictures

A modern language model has billions of them. This chapter’s own figures show the scale: GPT-1 had 117 million parameters, GPT-2 had 1.5 billion and GPT-3 had 175 billion.[1][2][3] “Parameters” is the usual word for the full count of adjustable numbers, and weights are the main kind (Chapter 6 covers this, citing IBM).

The weights hold patterns learned from text rather than an indexed list of facts (a simplification, and general background). One consequence is that a model has a limited ability to look up or update what it knows, which section 5.4 covers,[10] and a fluent answer can still be wrong, as section 5.5 explains.[11] When a company publishes the file of weights, the model is called an open-weight model. Chapter 6 covers what that does and does not mean.

5.3

Becoming an assistant: fine-tuning and RLHF

A model that only predicts text is not yet an assistant. Asked a question, it may continue with more questions, because that is what text often does. OpenAI’s 2022 InstructGPT work described a three-step fix.[7] First, fine-tune the model on example answers written by humans. Second, have people rank several of the model’s answers and train a separate reward model to predict which ones people prefer. Third, use reinforcement learning, the trial-and-error method from Chapter 3, to push the model toward answers that score well. The overall method is called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF.[7][8]

The effect was large. Labelers preferred answers from a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model over those from the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, despite having more than 100 times fewer parameters.[7] ChatGPT launched on November 30, 2022, using GPT-3.5 tuned with both supervised learning and RLHF, and it was estimated to reach 100 million monthly active users about two months later.[9]

Pre-training gives it language. Human feedback gives it manners.

Known limits of learning from human feedback

Collecting good feedback is expensive. Results depend on the annotators, and a single reward function may not reflect different groups’ views. A model can also learn to produce answers that score well but are wrong, such as confident-sounding mistakes, because people have trouble spotting errors on hard tasks.[8] The ChatGPT entry adds that it tends to agree with users even when they are wrong.[9]

Why it matters in educationA chatbot’s friendly tone is trained in, not a sign of understanding. The same training that makes it helpful can make it eager to please.

From section 5.3RLHF

RLHF aligns a model with human preferences. A reward model is trained to represent those preferences, and it is then used to train the main model with reinforcement learning.[8]

  1. Collect preferences. Humans write sample answers (the fine-tuning step) and rank the model’s outputs.[8]
  2. Train a reward model. It learns to score answers the way people ranked them.[8]
  3. Optimize. The model is adjusted to earn higher scores, with a penalty that keeps it close to the original.[8]

5.4

Fresh information: retrieval-augmented generation

A trained model keeps its knowledge inside its parameters, and it stops learning when training ends. A 2020 paper by Patrick Lewis and colleagues called this stored knowledge parametric memory. It pointed out that models struggle to access it precisely, to explain where an answer came from, and to keep it up to date.[10]

The proposed fix is retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG. The system first searches an outside collection of documents, which the paper called non-parametric memory, and hands the best passages to the model along with the question. The model then writes its answer using them.[10] The paper reported state-of-the-art results on three open-domain question-answering tasks and more specific, factual text than a model without retrieval.[10]

An open-book exam instead of a closed-book one.

How it connects to what you have seen

The retrieved passages are placed in the model’s prompt, so they must fit inside its context window from Chapter 4. A passage that does not get retrieved is invisible to the model, which is why retrieval quality matters as much as the model.

What RAG does and does not fix

The paper lists explaining where an answer came from and updating knowledge as open problems for models that store everything in their weights, and it proposes retrieval as a hybrid approach.[10] It does not make a model infallible. The model can still misread or ignore what it was given, and the search can return the wrong passages.

Why it matters in educationMany school tools, such as a chatbot that answers from a district handbook, work this way. Ask where an answer came from. If the tool can point to a passage, it probably retrieved one.

5.5

Why hallucination happens

An AI hallucination is a response that contains false or misleading information presented as fact. It is also called confabulation.[11] The causes include gaps and errors in the training data, and the way these models are built: next-word pre-training pushes a model to give a guess even when it lacks the information. OpenAI researchers have also argued that common training and evaluation methods reward guessing over admitting uncertainty.[11]

Real cases are well documented. In 2023 a lawyer in Mata v. Avianca filed six court precedents that ChatGPT had invented, and the judge fined the lawyers $5,000 in total. In February 2024 a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay damages and honor a bereavement fare policy that its support chatbot had made up.[11]

The model is built to continue text plausibly. Being right is a separate skill.

At 0% the model answers everything and makes four mistakes. Raising the cutoff removes mistakes but also removes some correct answers. If a test gives no credit for “I don’t know,” guessing always scores at least as well, which is the incentive problem described above.

Not just randomness

Randomness in word choice can make a model less consistent, but a hallucination is not only a result of turning that setting up. The model can be confidently wrong with the randomness low. The next explainer shows what the setting does.

What helps

The Wikipedia overview lists cleaner and more faithful datasets, adding outside information to the input, reinforcement learning, scoring several candidate answers for confidence, and guardrail tools.[11] InstructGPT’s tuning roughly halved invented details on closed-domain tasks, from 41% to 21%.[7] None of these removes the problem.

Why it matters in educationFluent and correct are different things. A good habit for students is to check any specific name, number, date or citation a chatbot gives, because those are the places where invention hides.

From section 5.5Temperature

Temperature is a setting that controls how random a language model’s word choice is during inference. The model gives each candidate word a score, softmax (Chapter 4) turns the scores into probabilities, and temperature reshapes those probabilities before a word is picked. Low temperature concentrates probability on the likeliest words, and high temperature gives less likely words a better chance.[14]

From section 5.5Top-P

Top-P, also called nucleus sampling, is a second setting that shapes a language model’s word choice. The model scores every candidate word and softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Top-P then sorts the words from most to least likely and keeps the smallest group whose probabilities add up to P, for example 0.9, which means 90%. The next word is picked only from that group, so the long tail of unlikely words is cut off.[15][16]

The P is a share of probability, not a p-value from statistics. Holtzman and colleagues proposed the method in a paper first posted in 2019 and published at ICLR 2020. They describe it as sampling from the dynamic nucleus of the probability distribution, which cuts off the less reliable tail.[15]

Temperature and Top-P are separate settings: one reshapes the probabilities, the other trims the tail. How they interact depends on the software you use, so check its documentation before combining them. Like temperature, Top-P changes how varied the answers are. It does not make a model know more.

5.6

Images from noise: diffusion

Chatbots write text, but 2022 also made image generators popular, and most of them use diffusion. Training starts with real images and adds a little random noise, step after step, until only static remains. A neural network then learns to undo one step of noise at a time.[13] To make a new image, the system starts from pure random noise and applies the trained network repeatedly until a picture emerges.[13]

Text prompts steer the process. The prompt is turned into vectors, the same idea as Chapter 4’s embeddings, and the denoising network attends to them while it works. Stable Diffusion, released in August 2022, does this with cross-attention layers. DALL-E 2, from April 2022, used a different text-to-image mapping.[13] A key paper from June 2020 described these models and reported a state-of-the-art image-quality score on the CIFAR10 benchmark.[12]

Learn to clean up static, then start from static and clean it up into something new.

How many steps?

The original method used about 1,000 denoising steps, which is slow. A faster variant called DDIM can skip steps, and the Wikipedia entry reports that 20 steps give samples that look broadly similar to 1,000-step ones.[13]

Sizes

The Wikipedia entry describes DALL-E 2 as a 3.5-billion-parameter cascaded diffusion model (it gives the same figure for an earlier model, GLIDE, so treat it as approximate), and Stable Diffusion has a denoising network of about 860 million parameters, working in a compressed “latent” image space.[13]

Why it matters in educationDiffusion means these images are generated fresh from noise and a prompt, not cut and pasted from existing pictures. That is a useful starting point for conversations about style, copyright and what counts as someone’s work.

Explainers

From section 5.6Diffusion

A diffusion model generates data by reversing a gradual noising process. In training it adds noise to real examples in many small steps. It learns to undo one step, then generates by starting from noise and denoising repeatedly.[12][13]

DirectionWhat happens
Forward (training data)A clean image is slowly turned into noise
Reverse (generation)Noise is slowly turned into an image, guided by the prompt

The 2020 paper describes diffusion models as latent variable models inspired by nonequilibrium thermodynamics.[12]
