7.1

Prompting and iteration

A prompt is whatever you give a chatbot to work from. Chapter 5 showed why wording matters: the model continues the text in front of it, and a few examples in that text can steer it. Anthropic’s prompting guidance lists a handful of habits. Be explicit, and ask for more than the literal task if you want it. Explain why you want something. Give three to five relevant examples. Assign a role in a sentence.[1]

One test is easy to remember. Show your prompt to a colleague who knows nothing about the task. If they would be confused, the model probably will be too.[1] Treat the first answer as a draft, not a verdict, and say what to change. That last habit is my advice, not a sourced finding.

A good prompt is a good brief: who it is for, what it is for, and what it should look like.

What the guidance says, in order of usefulness

HabitWhat it means
Be explicitIf you want more than the literal task, ask for it
Explain whyGiving the reason behind an instruction helps the model apply it sensibly
Show examplesThree to five, relevant and varied, are among the most reliable ways to control format and tone

Source: Anthropic’s prompting guidance. It is written for one model family, so treat it as a good general guide, not a law.[1]

What prompting cannot fix

Careful wording does not make a model know what it does not know. Chapter 5’s lesson still applies: check specific names, dates, numbers and citations.

Why it matters in educationStudents who learn to write a clear brief are practicing a real skill: saying precisely what they want and why. That is a thinking task, not only a tool trick.

7.2

AI in the classroom: from the ban to a plan

Schools reacted to ChatGPT within weeks. On January 3, 2023, New York City’s Education Department blocked it on school devices and networks, citing negative impacts on student learning and concerns about the safety and accuracy of its content.[2] The block had limits. Schools could ask for access to study the technology, and it did not reach devices or networks outside the department, so students could still use the site elsewhere.[2] About four months later, the city was reported to have reversed course.[3]

Guidance since then has leaned toward a plan instead of a ban. UNESCO’s September 2023 guidance proposes a human-centred, age-appropriate approach, calls for regulation that protects users’ data privacy, and proposes an age limit for independent conversations with these tools. It also notes that institutions are largely unprepared to validate them.[10]

Blocking a tool at school does not remove it from students’ lives.

The reversal, in one voice

Jason Yip, a University of Washington professor quoted in coverage of the reversal, said that banning ChatGPT is “like using a piece of paper to block this flood that is coming.”[3] That is one expert’s view. The block’s own stated concerns were learning, safety and accuracy.[2]

What a district plan usually has to cover

UNESCO’s guidance touches several of these: data privacy, an age limit for independent use, and whether institutions can validate the tools.[10] My own checklist adds approved tools, teacher training and how work is assessed. The next sections take learning evidence, assessment and privacy in turn.

Why it matters in educationA policy that only says “don’t” invites quiet use. A policy that says what the tool is for, and what the student must still do alone, is easier to follow and to explain to families.
7.3

What the learning evidence says

The evidence is early, and two studies point the same way. In an experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey, one group practiced with a ChatGPT-style helper, one with a tutor version that gave hints instead of answers, and one with no AI. During practice, the first group did 48% better than the control group and the tutor group did 127% better. Then the AI was taken away for an exam. The first group scored 17% worse than the control group, and the tutor group scored about the same.[4]

The authors concluded that AI can make a task easier while weakening the underlying skill, and that students were overly optimistic about how much they had learned. In their reading, the tutor version, with hints and teacher input, avoided the exam penalty.[4] A separate MIT Media Lab preprint with 54 participants found that essay writers using a chatbot had the weakest brain connectivity on EEG, the lowest sense of owning their essay and difficulty quoting it afterward, compared with people writing unaided.[5]

AI can make the task easier while weakening the skill the task was meant to build.

How to read this carefully

StudyWhat it can tell youWhat it cannot
Turkish high school math[4]A design with a control group, so a fair comparison of tool designsWhether the same holds for other subjects, ages or countries
MIT essay-writing preprint[5]That the tool changed how people wrote, remembered and felt about the work in that settingLasting harm. It is a small preprint, and brain connectivity is not the same as learning

The last column is my reading of what each study can support. The Wharton article quotes one of the authors, Bastani, saying AI can be a big benefit when people treat it as an assistant and check its outputs, and that trouble starts when they trust it completely.[4]

The MIT study’s design

The 54 participants wrote essays under one of three conditions: a chatbot, a search engine, or no tools. In a fourth session, 18 of them were reassigned, with chatbot users moving to no tools and the reverse. Former chatbot users showed reduced alpha and beta connectivity, and former no-tool writers showed higher memory recall, similar to search-engine users.[5]

Why it matters in educationThe design of the tool, and of the task, seems to matter more than whether AI is present. Hints and checks looked safer than answers, and a no-AI exam is the check that shows what a student can do alone.
7.4

Assessment redesign, and why detectors fail

It is tempting to answer AI with an AI detector. The evidence says not to. OpenAI retired its own detector on July 20, 2023 “due to its low rate of accuracy,” after warning at launch that it was impossible to reliably detect all AI-written text.[7] A 2023 study tested several widely used detectors and found they consistently misclassified writing by non-native English speakers as AI-generated.[6]

The numbers are stark. On 91 essays written by non-native English speakers, the detectors’ average false positive rate was 61.22%. All seven detectors agreed that 18 essays were AI-written, and at least one detector flagged 89 of the 91.[6] The authors cautioned against using these detectors in evaluative or educational settings.[6]

A detector that wrongly accuses the students it should protect is worse than none.

The rewrite prompt asked a chatbot to enhance the word choices to sound more like a native speaker. Only 1 of 91 essays was then flagged by all detectors. Two facts follow. Detectors wrongly flag honest writers, and a trivial rewrite defeats them. The same paper reports that simple prompting strategies can bypass the detectors.[6]

7.5

Risks: bias, privacy, prompt injection and over-attachment

Four risks matter most for schools. Bias is a repeatable tendency to produce unfair outcomes. It can come from the training data, from design choices and from how a tool is used.[8] Documented examples include a recruiting tool Amazon turned off in 2015 after it was found to be biased against women, and a 2018 study that found commercial face-recognition error rates up to 35% for darker-skinned women against under 1% for lighter-skinned men. The same article notes that language models often present Anglo-American views as truth.[8]

Privacy comes next. In the United States, FERPA protects education records and the personal information in them, and parents hold those rights until a student turns 18 or enters a postsecondary school.[11] COPPA covers children under 13: operators generally need verifiable parental consent before collecting their personal information.[12] I did not read the part of the FTC’s guidance on whether schools can consent for parents, so that question needs your district’s legal advice.

Prompt injection is an attack that hides instructions in content a model reads, such as a web page, a file or a résumé. The model treats them as commands, and a person may never see them.[13] Over-attachment is the fourth. The American Psychological Association’s June 2025 advisory warns that adolescents may trust AI companions too readily, may struggle to tell simulated empathy from real understanding, and that heavy attachment could interfere with real-world relationships.[9]

Treat every chatbot as a stranger who is very good at sounding like a friend.

Bias in the sources

Bias is hard to detect for several reasons: the systems are complex and often proprietary, protected attributes are often not collected and proxies can carry bias anyway, and fairness has no single definition.[8] Chapter 5 reported that the InstructGPT tuning showed no significant improvement on two bias benchmarks.

What the APA recommends

The advisory recommends teaching young people that AI can be inaccurate, biased and persuasive and can encourage overreliance, building that literacy into core curricula, protecting young people’s data and likeness (including from deepfakes), and reminding users that they are talking to a bot.[9]

Why it matters in educationBefore a student or teacher types a name, a grade or a diagnosis into any tool, ask where the text goes and who can read it. Prefer tools the district has reviewed.

From section 7.5Prompt injection

Prompt injection manipulates a model into carrying out an attacker’s goals without the user noticing. In the direct kind, a user overwrites the system prompt. In the indirect kind, hidden instructions sit in outside content the model processes, such as a web page, a file or a résumé, and they need not be visible to a person.[13]

ImpactExample
Data leakRevealing a system prompt or sensitive data

7.6

Outlook: another winter, or an eternal spring?

Chapter 2 told the story of two AI winters, and the history of forecasts is part of that story. Artificial general intelligence, or AGI, is a hypothetical AI that matches or surpasses humans across virtually all cognitive tasks. It differs from the narrow AI that handles a defined task. There is no single agreed definition of intelligence for computers, and so none for AGI.[14]

Predictions vary widely. In 1965 Herbert Simon predicted machines would do any work a human could within twenty years, and that failed. An analysis of 95 predictions made between 1950 and 2012 found a pattern of expecting human-level AI roughly 15 to 25 years away, whenever the prediction was made. In 2012–2013 polls, experts’ median estimate was 2040 to 2050, with 16.5% answering “never” at 90% confidence. Individual voices range from Geoffrey Hinton’s 5 to 20 years, given with low confidence in 2024, to Paul Allen’s 2011 view that AGI was unlikely this century.[14]

Hold forecasts loosely. The honest answer is that nobody knows.

Claims that AGI already exists

Some people say it is already here. Microsoft researchers in 2023 wrote that GPT-4 could be viewed as an early, incomplete version of AGI, and others have argued it is already here. Critics note that the public often perceives today’s chatbots as AGI, which fuels misunderstanding.[14] Because definitions differ, these claims are partly arguments about words.

The risk debate

Whether AGI poses an existential risk is contested. A 2023 joint statement by the leaders of Google DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic said that mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority, while skeptics say AGI is far off or that such talk distracts from current harms. Andrew Ng has suggested some risk campaigns may serve regulatory capture.[14] This chapter does not take a side. For a classroom, the near-term risks above are the ones you can act on.

Why it matters in educationStudents will hear confident claims in both directions. A useful habit is to ask who is making the forecast, what they gain from it, and how past forecasts by anyone have fared.
